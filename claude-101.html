What is Claude

Claude is more than a chatbot — it's an AI assistant designed to be your thinking partner. This lesson covers what makes Claude different from other AI tools, and how it can help across a wide variety of work tasks.

By the end of this lesson

  • Identify the different ways to access Claude (web, desktop, and mobile)

Key takeaways

Claude is built to be helpful, harmless, and honest.
Claude is guided by principles that help it avoid toxic or discriminatory outputs, avoid helping humans engage in illegal or unethical activities, and broadly behave as a helpful, honest, and harmless AI system. This approach, called Constitutional AI, means Claude is trained to align with human values and operate transparently.
Claude is more than a chatbot.
It handles a wide variety of conversational and text-processing tasks while staying reliable and predictable — summarization, search, creative and collaborative writing, Q&A, coding, and more. Think of Claude as a thinking partner for complex problems, not just an answer machine for simple questions.
Claude is designed to be steerable and collaborative.
Claude takes direction on personality, tone, and behavior. Customers report that Claude is much less likely to produce harmful outputs, easier to converse with, and more steerable — so you reach your desired output with less effort.
You can access Claude wherever you work.
Claude apps are available on Free, Pro, Max, Team, and Enterprise. Conversations, projects, memory, and preferences sync across all devices when you're signed in — web, desktop, and mobile.

Sort six requests

Six requests. Three belong in a search box, three need a thinking partner. The line was never the topic — reviews, invoices, and exchange rates could all be typed into a search box. What separates the columns is whether the answer needs you in it: your draft, your last deck, your read on the customer.

A search box handles theseThese need Claude — and you
"What's the exchange rate from US dollars to euros today?""Rewrite my update below so the delay reads as a decision, not an apology."
"What's the difference between a purchase order and an invoice?""Best structure for a quarterly business review presentation"
"How many working days are there between March 3 and March 24?""I've pasted a customer's complaint thread below. Help me work out what they're actually asking for before I reply."

A search box takes a question. Claude takes the situation. The two that trip most people are the review structure and the invoice question — same subject matter, but one is typed as a query and the other handed over as a situation.

A few things Claude excels at

  • Writing and content creation. Social posts, professional emails, complex reports. Because Claude takes direction on personality and tone, you can iterate together on structure and clarity until your voice comes through.
  • Research and analysis. Explore research angles, compile findings, analyze data. Claude's large context window ingests 200K+ tokens — about 500 pages of text or more — with up to 1M tokens on Pro, Max, Team, and Enterprise plans using supported models.
  • Coding assistance. One of Claude's greatest strengths: writing, debugging, and explaining code across many languages.
  • Problem-solving and reasoning. Complex cognitive tasks, mathematics, strategic analysis. Claude can respond near-instantly or take time to reason first — a capability called Thinking.
  • Learning new things. Claude adapts to your learning style and pace. Learning mode guides your reasoning rather than handing over answers, building critical thinking instead of dependence.

Your first conversation with Claude

By the end of this lesson

Key takeaways

  • Claude amplifies your capabilities across all of your work. Claude brings AI intelligence; you bring the context and expertise that makes the work meaningful.
  • The best approach is to speak to Claude the way you would a coworker — naturally, concisely, conversationally.
  • Before your next conversation, consider setting the stage (role, objectives, context), defining the task (the action you want), and specifying rules (style, tone, examples).
  • When you upload relevant documents, Claude considers that content in its response — a shortcut to understanding what you need.
  • The real power comes from continued, frequent communication, not one-off prompts.

Writing effective prompts

Every interaction begins with a prompt, and the prompt plus surrounding context shapes the response. Prompts range from a simple question — brainstorming code names for a feature — to a complex request to co-create files. Before your next conversation, consider three things:

Setting the stage
What is your role and what are your objectives? Is there context about your work Claude should know?
Defining the task
What action do you want Claude to take — write, analyze, build, something else?
Specifying rules
What style or tone do you want? Are there examples you can attach to show what you're looking for?

Putting it together

"I'm the marketing lead at an indie streaming startup, and we're preparing an investor pitch deck for Series A investors. Can you research the current state of the independent film streaming market and identify key trends, competitor positioning, and growth opportunities? Use current web research with citations and structure it as a professional report of up to 5 pages, with an executive summary, market analysis, competitive landscape, and growth opportunities."
  • Setting the stage — an investor pitch deck for a new indie streaming app: the context and objective.
  • Defining the task — research the market, with the details that matter: trends, competitors, opportunities.
  • Specifying rules — current web research with citations, structured as a professional report of a stated length.

Adding context

Uploads, connectors, and custom preferences all give Claude more context about your work. Claude analyzes both text and visual elements — images, charts, graphics — in PDFs and other documents. Supported types include PDF, DOCX, CSV, TXT, and common image formats like PNG and JPEG.

  • Upload a document and ask Claude to summarize the key points
  • Share an image and ask Claude to describe or analyze what it sees
  • Attach a spreadsheet and ask Claude to identify trends in the data
  • Upload code and ask Claude to explain how it works or find bugs

Once uploaded, Claude parses the file automatically; it appears in the chat as an attachment you can then prompt about.

Pro tip

For preferences you want in every response, go to Settings → General → "What personal preferences should Claude consider?"

Iterating on Claude's responses

Conversations are meant to be iterative. Chaining bite-sized prompts creates a dialogue you steer based on what Claude says. If the first response isn't right:

  • Ask follow-up questions. "Can you expand on the second point?" or "That's helpful, but can you make it more concise?"
  • Provide feedback. "This is good, but the tone is too formal. Can you make it more conversational?"
  • Redirect or restart. "Actually, I was asking about X, not Y." Worst case, open a new chat to fully refresh the context.
Pro tip

Click the pencil icon on any of your own messages to edit and resubmit the prompt — useful when you want to refine the request rather than add a new one.

Personalizing Claude

Memory

Saves key context from your conversations — role, preferences, past decisions, working style — so you don't repeat yourself in every new chat. Tell Claude you work in marketing at a B2B company and it carries that forward. Review, edit, or delete anything it remembers in Settings; memory syncs across devices.

Styles

Customize how Claude communicates. Choose a preset — concise, formal, explanatory — or create a custom style by describing exactly how you want Claude to write. Once set, it applies across all conversations automatically.

The iteration mindset

One of the most important shifts when working with Claude is recognizing that your first prompt rarely produces a perfect result — and that's okay. Think of your initial prompt as the start of a conversation, not a one-shot request. Effective Claude users:

  • Treat first drafts as starting points. Review what Claude produces, identify what's working and what isn't, then refine.
  • Give specific feedback. "Make it shorter" is fine, but "Cut the first two paragraphs and make the conclusion more action-oriented" is better.
  • Know when to start fresh. If a conversation has gone off track, sometimes it's faster to open a new chat with a clearer prompt than to try to redirect.

What is AI Fluency?

AI Fluency is the ability to collaborate effectively with AI tools — not just knowing which buttons to click, but developing the judgment to use AI well across different situations. The 4D Framework for AI Fluency, developed through research collaboration between Professor Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design) and Professor Joseph Feller (University College Cork), identifies four core competencies:

Delegation
Deciding what work should be done by humans, what should be done by AI, and how to distribute tasks between them.
Description
Effectively communicating with AI systems — clearly defining outputs, guiding AI processes, specifying desired behaviors.
Discernment
Thoughtfully and critically evaluating AI outputs, processes, and behaviors — assessing quality, accuracy, and appropriateness.
Diligence
Using AI responsibly and ethically — maintaining transparency and taking accountability for AI-assisted work.

You've already been practicing these skills. The prompt framework from the previous lesson (setting the stage, defining the task, specifying rules) is rooted in Description. The troubleshooting techniques above draw on Discernment and Diligence.

Evaluating Claude for your workflows

As you integrate Claude into more of your work, you might wonder: how do I know if Claude is actually good at this particular task? This is where Discernment becomes essential. Evals (short for evaluations) are systematic ways to test how well Claude performs on specific types of tasks that matter to you. Running simple evals helps you:

  • Understand where Claude adds the most value in your workflow
  • Identify tasks where you'll need to provide more context or examples
  • Build confidence in Claude's outputs for recurring tasks

A simple eval approach, no complex infrastructure required:

  • Gather examples. Collect 5–10 examples of a task you do regularly — emails, reports, analyses.
  • Create test prompts. Write prompts that would generate similar outputs, including the context you'd naturally have.

What is a project?

A project is a dedicated workspace that keeps everything related to one ongoing effort together — conversations, files, and instructions — so you don't have to re-explain the context every time you start a new chat. Every conversation you have inside a project can see the same shared knowledge, which means Claude already knows the background before you ask the first question.

Think of a project as the folder you'd create for a piece of work anyway — a client account, a quarterly report, a product launch — except Claude actually reads what's inside it.

What goes into a project

Custom instructions
Standing guidance for how Claude should behave inside this project — tone, format preferences, things to always check, a house style. Set once, applied to every conversation in the project.
Project knowledge
Files you upload — briefs, style guides, past reports, spreadsheets, transcripts — that Claude treats as background it already knows, without you re-attaching them each time.
Conversations
Every chat started inside the project lives there, so earlier threads on the same effort stay findable and connected instead of scattering across your history.

When to reach for a project

Start a project when the work is ongoing rather than a single question — anything you'll come back to across multiple sessions, with a stable set of background material. A few signs it's time:

  • You catch yourself re-pasting the same background information into new chats
  • Several people on a team need to work from the same shared context
  • The work has its own reference material — a style guide, a dataset, past decisions — that should inform every conversation about it
  • You expect to return to this work over days or weeks, not just today

For a one-off question or a task with no ongoing context to preserve, a regular chat is simpler — there's no need to set up a project for something you'll only ask once.

What is an artifact?

An artifact is a substantial, standalone piece of content that Claude creates alongside the conversation, in its own panel — a document, a piece of code, a diagram, a spreadsheet, a small interactive app. Instead of scrolling back through chat history to find the current version of what you're working on, the artifact sits in its own space, and Claude updates it in place as you iterate.

The distinction that matters: if what Claude produces is meant to be read once as part of the answer, it stays in the chat. If it's something you'll keep, edit, reuse, or share as a standalone piece of work, it becomes an artifact.

What works well as an artifact

  • Documents — reports, briefs, plans, structured write-ups you'll keep and revise
  • Code — scripts, components, or small tools, especially anything you'll run or reuse
  • Visuals — diagrams, charts, and simple interactive interfaces built from real content
  • Structured data — tables and spreadsheet-style content you'll want to keep referencing

Iterating on an artifact

Once an artifact exists, you keep working on it the same way you'd keep working with Claude turn by turn: ask for a section rewritten, a chart restyled, a function fixed. Claude updates the artifact in place rather than starting over, and earlier versions stay available if you want to go back.

Pro tip

Be specific about what should change and what should stay the same — "keep the structure, just tighten the introduction" gets a cleaner result than "make it better."

Sharing an artifact

A finished artifact can be shared as a link, so someone without a Claude account can open and view it directly — useful for a report you want a colleague to read, or a small tool you want someone else to try, without exporting files back and forth.

What is a Skill?

A Skill is a packaged set of instructions — and optionally reference files or scripts — that teaches Claude how to carry out a specific, repeatable task exactly the way you want it done, every time. Where a project holds ongoing context for a body of work, a Skill holds a reusable procedure: the steps, the format, the conventions, captured once so you don't have to restate them.

Skills can be invoked directly, or Claude can recognize on its own that a Skill applies to what you're asking and use it automatically.

When a task is a good candidate for a Skill

  • You do the same kind of task repeatedly, with the same expected shape of output
  • There's a "right way" to do it — a house format, a checklist, a specific sequence of steps
  • You want the same behavior available across different conversations and projects, not tied to one workspace
  • The task is precise enough to describe as a procedure, rather than open-ended judgment

Connecting your tools

By the end of this lesson

Why connect your tools

On its own, Claude only knows what you tell it in the conversation. A connector links Claude to a tool you already use — a calendar, a docs library, a project tracker, a messaging app — so Claude can pull in real, current information from that tool instead of relying on what you paste in or remember. The difference is between describing your calendar to Claude and letting Claude actually look at it.

Once a tool is connected, Claude can reference it the same way a colleague with access would: checking what's already there, pulling the right document, or looking up a fact, without you needing to go find it and copy it over first.

MCP: the standard behind connectors

Connectors are built on the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard for linking AI systems to external tools and data sources. Because it's a shared standard rather than a one-off integration, MCP means new tools can be connected in a consistent way, and connections you set up keep working as both the tools and Claude evolve.

Good candidates to connect

  • Tools that hold information you reference constantly — a shared drive, a wiki, a ticketing system
  • Tools where "current" matters — a calendar, a live project board, a support inbox
  • Tools you'd otherwise manually copy information out of, over and over

Enterprise search

What is Enterprise Search?

Enterprise Search lets Claude search across all of an organization's connected tools at once — company documents, chat history, tickets, wikis — instead of you having to know which single system holds the answer and open a connector to it directly. Where a single connector is one door into one tool, Enterprise Search is one question asked across every door your organization has opened.

This matters because the answer to a real work question often isn't sitting in one place — the decision was made in a meeting summarized in chat, documented in a wiki page, and referenced again in a ticket. Enterprise Search finds it wherever it landed.

What it's built to answer

  • "What did we decide about X?" — when the decision could be in email, chat, or a doc, and you don't know which
  • "Has anyone already written about Y?" — before duplicating work that might already exist somewhere in the organization
  • "Who owns Z?" — when the answer depends on org knowledge scattered across systems rather than a single source
In the product today

Enterprise Search is available on plans with the connected data sources an organization has enabled; what it can search depends on what your organization has connected.

Lesson 3.3

Research for deep dives

By the end of this lesson

  • Recognize when a question calls for Research rather than a faster tool
  • Route a set of realistic questions to the right tool: Research, quick web search, Thinking, or Enterprise Search

What is Research?

Research is built for open-ended questions that need real investigation — Claude plans a research approach, searches across many sources, follows leads as they turn up, and synthesizes what it finds into a structured answer with citations. It's built for depth and breadth: minutes of investigation instead of a single lookup, across dozens of sources instead of the first few results.

This is a different tool from a quick web search, which answers a question that has one clear, current answer somewhere on the web. Research is for the question that doesn't have one answer waiting — it has to be built by gathering, comparing, and weighing multiple sources.

When to reach for Research

  • The question requires synthesizing information from multiple, possibly conflicting sources
  • You need breadth — a landscape view of a topic, not one fact
  • The answer benefits from citations you can go verify yourself
  • You have a few minutes to let Claude work, rather than needing an instant answer

Reach for a quick web search instead when you need one current fact fast — today's exchange rate, whether a store is open, the latest version number of a tool.
